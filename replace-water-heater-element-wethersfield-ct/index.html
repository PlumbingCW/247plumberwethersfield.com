Water Heater Element Swap Without Draining: Wethersfield CT

You can swap a failed water heater element, but the tank water has to drop below it first and the power has to be off. Here is the safe order of work.

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

A failed heating element is one of the most common reasons an electric water heater goes cold, and replacing one is a job many homeowners can do in under an hour. The element is a small part held in the side of the tank by a threaded fitting or a bolted flange. It is cheap compared with the heater.

The title promises a swap without draining, so here is the honest version. You do not always need to empty the whole tank, but the water level has to fall below the element you are changing, and that takes a partial drain. Skipping that step floods the floor and exposes you to live wiring.

Signs an element has failed

No hot water at all

If both elements fail, or the upper one fails first, the tank may stay cold.

Lukewarm water that runs out fast

A failed lower element leaves only the top third of the tank hot.

A tripped breaker that trips again

A shorted element can trip the breaker as soon as it is reset.

Water that heats slowly

A scaled or weak element takes much longer to bring the tank up to temperature.

Test before you buy

Do not assume the element is the problem. A tripped high limit switch, a dead thermostat or a breaker that is off can all give the same symptoms. A multimeter on the resistance setting tells you quickly. With the power off at the breaker and the wires removed, a good element reads a low, steady resistance. A reading of infinity means the element is open and needs replacing.

Prove the power is off

Turn off the breaker, then confirm with a non-contact tester or a meter at the heater terminals. A breaker labeled wrong, or a heater fed from two breakers, is how people get hurt on this job.

Replace the element in order

1

Cut the power

Switch off the breaker and confirm no voltage at the terminals.

2

Shut off the water

Close the cold supply valve at the top of the heater.

3

Lower the water level

Attach a hose to the drain valve and open a hot tap upstairs. Drain until the water is below the element, which for the lower element means draining most of the tank.

4

Remove the old element

Take off the access cover and insulation, disconnect the wires and unscrew the element with an element wrench.

5

Fit the new element

Match the voltage and wattage printed on the old one, use the new gasket and tighten the element snugly.

6

Refill before power

Close the drain, open the cold valve and run a hot tap until the water flows steadily. Only then restore the power.

That last step matters more than any other. An element that is powered while the tank is empty burns out in seconds. Run the hot tap until you get a steady stream with no sputtering, then switch the breaker on.

When to stop and call a plumber

  • The tank leaks from the body, not from the element fitting.
  • The element is seized and will not turn.
  • The wiring is scorched or brittle.
  • You cannot confirm the power is off.
  • The heater is more than a decade old and rusted at the fittings.

An old tank with a new element is still an old tank. If the heater is near the end of its life, a water heater replacement may be a better use of money than another repair. Why elements fail

Elements fail for three main reasons. Sediment and mineral scale build up on the element surface and make it run hotter than it should, which shortens its life. A dry fire, caused by running the element with no water around it, burns it out in seconds. And age does the rest, because heating and cooling cycles fatigue the metal. Soft water slows scale, but it does not remove the risk.

Flushing the tank while you are at it

If you are already draining the tank for an element swap, it is a good time to flush the sediment. Open the drain valve fully, let the water run until it clears and give the tank a quick stir with the cold supply to dislodge what is left. A clean tank heats more evenly and loads the elements less, so the new one lasts longer.

Check the anode rod

While the tank is down, ask whether the anode rod has been checked. It is the sacrificial part that protects the tank from rust, and replacing it is far cheaper than replacing the heater.

If the tank leaks from the body or the bottom, the tank has failed, and no element will fix it. Look for rust streaks and damp insulation around the base before you buy parts.

Code and permit note

A like for like element swap is maintenance. Electrical changes and a new heater are different, and the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), as adopted by Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code, governs the plumbing side of a replacement.
