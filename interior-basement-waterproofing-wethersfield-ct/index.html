Interior Basement Waterproofing in Wethersfield, CT Explained

Interior waterproofing collects water that gets in and sends it to a sump pump. It manages water, but it does not stop it at the wall.

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

Interior basement waterproofing works by catching water that seeps through the wall or floor joint and carrying it to a pit. A perimeter drain runs along the footing inside the basement, a sump pump removes the water and the walls are often sealed or covered with a membrane. It is usually less disruptive than digging up the outside of the house.

This guide explains how the system works, what it costs you in disruption, what it cannot do and how to decide between interior and exterior approaches.

How an interior system works

1

Open the floor edge

A strip of slab along the walls is cut and removed.

2

Lay the drain

A perforated pipe goes in a bed of gravel along the footing.

3

Connect to the pit

The drain slopes to a sump pit where a pump sits.

4

Cover and finish

The slab is patched with concrete and a wall membrane directs seepage to the drain.

Signs you need it

Water along the wall base after rain

Seepage at the floor and wall joint means hydrostatic pressure is pushing in.

White powder on the wall

Efflorescence shows water moving through the masonry.

Musty smell

Constant dampness feeds mold and mildew.

Cracks that leak

A leaking crack may need sealing in addition to drainage.

What interior waterproofing cannot do

It does not stop water from reaching the wall. It gives the water a place to go. If the wall itself is failing, if a crack is moving or if the yard drains toward the house, those problems need their own fix. Gutters, grading and downspout extensions often solve more than people expect, and they cost less than any system.

Our basement waterproofing service starts with a diagnosis, not a package. A pump is part of most systems, and our sump pump service covers it. We serve Newington and Hartford along with Wethersfield, and the emergency line is answered 24/7 if water is coming in now.

Water, the Cove and the sewer in Wethersfield

The town's 2013 Plan of Conservation and Development notes that the flood of 1692 may have created the Cove, which shows how closely Wethersfield has lived with the Connecticut River. Much of the town sits on low ground.

MDC's Clean Water Project adds a second source of basement water. It says sewer flow rises from about 1.7 million gallons a day to about 9.1 million in rain, and that overflows can reach basements and Wethersfield Cove. If your basement water arrives through a floor drain, a backflow device and a plumber's camera matter more than a wall membrane.

Source type: Wethersfield 2013 Plan of Conservation and Development, MDC Clean Water Project.

Fixes that cost less than a system

Before paying for a drain and a pump, check the outside. Gutters that overflow, downspouts that end at the foundation and soil that slopes toward the house put water right where you do not want it. Extending downspouts several feet from the wall, cleaning gutters and regrading the first few feet of soil often cut basement dampness a great deal.

Cracks need their own look. A hairline crack that seeps after a storm can be sealed from the inside. A crack that is widening, or a wall that bows inward, is a structural matter and needs an engineer, not a drain. A plumber who recommends a system without looking at the outside first has skipped a step.

What the job looks like inside the house

1

Protect the space

The crew covers or clears the basement and sets up dust control.

2

Cut and dig

A strip of floor along the walls is cut, broken out and dug to the footing.

3

Lay drain and gravel

Pipe is placed on the footing and covered with stone.

4

Set the pit and pump

The sump pit goes in at the low point and the discharge is routed outside.

5

Patch and test

Concrete closes the cut, and the system is tested with water before the job ends.

Expect noise, dust and a few days of work in the lower level. Move stored items out of the way before the crew arrives.

What to do if water is rising now

Standing water in the basement

Water and electricity do not mix, so safety comes before cleanup.

  • Stay out of the water if it is near outlets, the panel or any plugged in appliance.
  • Do not touch the electrical panel with wet hands or while standing in water.
  • Call the emergency line and describe where the water is coming from.
  • If the panel is in the water, call the electric utility and keep everyone upstairs.
  • Move valuables up only when the floor is dry and the power is off.

Planning around the weather

Dig and drain work goes best in dry conditions, but waterproofing is often done because the basement just flooded, so crews plan for wet days. Ask the contractor how they will handle water during the job, where the pump will discharge while the work is under way and how they protect the floor area from dust. A plain answer shows experience.

Ask about the warranty, and read what it covers. Some cover the drain and the pump for a set period. Others exclude anything that is not caused by a failure of the system itself. A plumber should walk you through what is and is not covered, and explain what maintenance you need to keep it valid.

Finally, ask what the crew will do about the sump pump discharge, the electrical supply and the floor finish. Those three details are where a good job and a rushed one differ most, and a contractor who has done many basements will have ready answers.

Keep your own records. Photograph the basement before the work, and note where moisture appeared and when. If a problem returns, that record helps a technician see what changed.

Code and permit note

An interior drain system cuts the slab and ties into a sump, so it is plumbing work. Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), and permit rules vary with the work.
