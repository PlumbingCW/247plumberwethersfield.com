Plumbing Pipe Fitting Types Explained for Wethersfield, CT

Pipe fittings join, turn, split, reduce or cap a line. The right one depends on the pipe material, the job and the code that applies.

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

A fitting is any part that connects pipe to pipe or pipe to a fixture. Elbows turn a line, tees split it, couplings join two lengths, unions let you take a line apart later and caps seal the end. Each comes in versions made for copper, plastic or steel, and mixing the wrong ones is behind many slow leaks.

This guide explains the common fitting types, which materials use which joint and where a homeowner can safely work. It also covers when the job belongs to a licensed plumber and how to check a license in Connecticut.

The main fitting shapes

Common fittings and what they do
FittingWhat it doesWhere you see it
CouplingJoins two straight pieces of pipeRepairs and extensions
Elbow (90 or 45 degrees)Changes the direction of the lineCorners, around obstacles
TeeBranches a line in a third directionSupply to a new fixture
UnionJoins pipe so it can be disconnectedWater heaters, pumps
AdapterJoins two different pipe types or threadsPlastic to metal transitions
ReducerSteps down to a smaller pipeBranches to a sink
Cap or plugCloses the end of a lineAbandoned or future lines

Fittings by pipe material

Copper

Sweat fittings are soldered. Compression and push to connect fittings avoid the torch.

PEX

Uses crimp rings, clamp rings or expansion fittings, plus push to connect types.

PVC and CPVC

Solvent welded with primer and cement. CPVC is rated for hot water, standard PVC is not.

Galvanized steel

Threaded fittings, common in older houses and prone to corrosion inside.

Cast iron

Used for older drain lines, joined with no hub couplings or lead and oakum in very old work.

Why the right fitting matters

A fitting that does not match its pipe can hold for a month and then weep. A plastic fitting threaded onto a metal pipe too tightly can crack. Two different metals in contact, such as copper against galvanized steel, corrode each other unless a dielectric fitting separates them. These are not exotic problems. They show up in ordinary basement work when someone uses what was on the shelf.

What a homeowner can safely do

1

Identify the pipe

Look at the material, the size marked on the pipe and the fittings already in place.

2

Shut the water off

Close the nearest valve and open a tap to drain the line.

3

Pick a matching fitting

Match material, size and pressure rating. When in doubt, take a cut sample to the store.

4

Make a clean joint

Cut square, deburr, clean and follow the joint method for that material.

5

Test slowly

Open the valve gradually and check every joint for weeping before you close the wall or cabinet.

Checking a plumber's license in Connecticut

The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection runs a license lookup at elicense.ct.gov. You can search a name or a license number and confirm the status before anyone cuts into a line.

That check is worth doing for any job beyond a simple repair. In Wethersfield, the Building Department at 505 Silas Deane Highway also handles permits, and permit rules vary with the work. A licensed plumber who pulls the permit protects you and your insurance if something fails later.

Pressure, temperature and code ratings

Fittings carry ratings for pressure and temperature, and the markings are stamped or printed on the body. A fitting rated for cold water is not safe on a hot line, and a fitting meant for drains cannot hold supply pressure. Reading the marks takes seconds and prevents a common failure.

The same goes for gas. Gas piping uses its own materials and its own joints, and it is regulated for good reason. A fitting that looks identical to a water fitting may not be approved for gas. Gas lines are never a homeowner project, and we say so plainly.

A short buying checklist

  • Match the pipe material and the nominal size.
  • Check the pressure and temperature rating on the fitting.
  • Use a dielectric fitting when joining dissimilar metals.
  • Buy the correct primer and cement or flux for the material.
  • Keep the packaging, so you can match the part number if you need another.

One more habit saves a lot of trouble. Take a photo of the old joint before you take it apart. Plumbing parts that look alike on a shelf are not always the same, and a photo helps a counter clerk or a plumber match the part on the first try.

If you are unsure about a fitting, a ten minute call to a plumber costs far less than a flooded cabinet. Our team is happy to look at a photo of the joint and tell you what it is.

Code and permit note

Pipe and fitting choices are set by the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), which Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code adopts. The code lists approved materials for supply, drain and vent lines, which is why a fitting that fits is not always one that is allowed.
