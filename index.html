Local plumbing facts

What Wethersfield plumbing actually looks like

Wethersfield is mostly single-family housing. The town's 2013 plan counts 11,677 housing units in 2010, and the 2019 town profile puts about 79 percent of homes in owner hands. Owners usually call about the thing in front of them, a leaking tank, a slow main drain or a toilet that runs all night, so we start with that problem and tell you plainly what else we notice.

About 27 percent of the town's housing was built before 1950, and the 1950s added roughly one new house a day. That puts two plumbing eras side by side. Homes of the older era can still have original galvanized supply pipe or cast iron drains, and the only way to know is to look. Repiping and sewer camera inspections are the two jobs where age shows up most.

Your water comes from the Metropolitan District, and MDC states that its water has a hardness of about 1 grain per gallon, which is soft. Mineral scale is a smaller factor for water heater tanks here than in hard-water towns, so an early failure is more likely a corroded tank or a worn part. If brown water lasts more than a short while, report it to MDC, because it usually points to a disturbance in the main and not to your pipes.

MDC began replacing water mains, services and meter pits along Silas Deane Highway in April 2025, with completion projected for August 2026. If you live along that corridor, check themdc.org for current status, because new service connections can briefly change pressure or stir up sediment. After work like that, a leak check on your side of the meter is a sensible habit.

MDC's Clean Water Project says sewer flow in Wethersfield climbs from about 1.7 million gallons a day in dry weather to about 9.1 million in heavy rain, and that overflows can reach basements and Wethersfield Cove. That is why a working sump pump and sound basement drainage matter here, and why we ask about both when we visit a wet cellar.
