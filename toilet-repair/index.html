A toilet that is overflowing, or one that will not stop running, needs attention fast. Our line is answered 24/7. While you wait, turn the shutoff valve behind the toilet clockwise to stop the water, lift the tank lid and raise the float to stop the fill, and keep the lid on the bowl.

Most toilet problems are a part and not a new toilet. A flapper, a fill valve, a wax ring or a clog accounts for the majority. We tell you which one you have and fix it.

Act first

Overflow or leak: what to do in the first minute

Stop the water before anything else

Toilet water on a bathroom floor can reach the ceiling below.

  • Turn the shutoff valve behind the toilet clockwise until it stops.
  • If the valve is stuck, lift the float in the tank to halt the fill, or close the main valve.
  • Do not flush again. Each flush adds water to a full bowl.
  • If the water looks like sewage, avoid contact and keep children and pets out of the room.

What is wrong with your toilet

It runs on and on

A worn flapper or a fill valve that does not shut off lets water trickle into the bowl all day, which raises your bill.

Weak or slow flush

Mineral buildup in the rim jets or a partial clog reduces the force of the flush.

Leaks at the base

A failed wax ring or loose closet bolts lets water out at the floor, and it can rot the subfloor.

Rocking or loose

A toilet that moves on the floor has loose bolts or a damaged flange, and it will leak sooner or later.

How we fix a toilet

We start with the cheapest explanation. A flapper, a chain or a fill valve takes minutes to replace. A clog is cleared with a closet auger, which reaches the trap without scratching the porcelain. A leak at the base means we pull the toilet, replace the wax ring and check the flange before setting it again.

If the toilet is cracked, or if repeated repairs are not holding, we will tell you that a replacement is the sound choice. We install new toilets as well, and our plumbing installation page explains how.

A running toilet and your water bill

A toilet that runs can waste a large amount of water in a day, and the cost shows up on your next bill. A quick test is to drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait twenty minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking and needs replacing.

If your toilet is not the leak source and the bill is still high, it is worth a look at the rest of the house. Our leak detection service traces the rest of the system.

Repeat clogs in older homes

A toilet that clogs again soon after a clearing can have a drain problem beyond the bowl. About 27 percent of Wethersfield homes were built before 1950, and older drain lines can have roots, bellies or buildup. When a toilet clogs regularly, a drain cleaning with a camera look can find the cause.

MDC runs the town sewer, and its Clean Water Project notes that heavy rain can overwhelm the system. A toilet that backs up during a storm, with other drains gurgling too, may be reacting to the main and not to the bowl. We tell you which it is.

Code and permit note

Most toilet repairs are parts swaps and need no permit. A new toilet or a changed drain is different, and the 2022 Connecticut State Building Code, which adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), sets fixture and venting rules that apply to that work.
