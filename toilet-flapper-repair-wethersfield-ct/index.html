How to Replace a Toilet Flapper | Wethersfield, CT Guide

A running toilet is usually a worn flapper. Here is how to confirm it, swap it and know when to stop.

Wethersfield guideLocal facts

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

A toilet that runs on its own, or that refills every few minutes with no one using it, almost always has a worn flapper. The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. When it stops sealing, water slips into the bowl, the fill valve opens to replace it and the cycle repeats all day.

You can replace one in about fifteen minutes with no special tools. This guide shows how to confirm the flapper is the problem, how to choose the right replacement, and when a running toilet means something else.

How the flapper works

When you press the handle, a chain lifts the flapper and the tank empties into the bowl. The flapper falls back, seals the opening and the tank refills. Over the years, the rubber hardens, warps or gets coated with mineral film, and it no longer seals evenly.

Confirm the flapper is the problem

Take the tank lid off and drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank. Do not flush. Wait about twenty minutes. If color shows up in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper. If the bowl stays clear, look at the fill valve and the overflow tube instead.

Color in the bowl

The flapper is leaking. Replace it.

Water rising to the top of the overflow tube

The fill valve is not shutting off, and the flapper is not the cause.

A chain that is too tight or too loose

A tight chain holds the flapper open. A loose one can snag under it. Adjust it so there is a little slack.

A hissing sound with no running water

The fill valve is worn. Replace it as a separate repair.

Replace the flapper in five steps

1

Shut off the water

Turn the valve behind the toilet clockwise, then flush to empty the tank.

2

Remove the old flapper

Unhook the chain and lift the ears off the pegs on the overflow tube.

3

Match the new one

Take the old flapper to the store. The size, often two or three inches, has to match your flush valve.

4

Install and adjust

Snap the new flapper on, hook the chain with a little slack and trim any extra.

5

Test

Open the valve, let the tank fill and flush a few times. Repeat the dye test the next day.

Choosing the right replacement

Flappers come in several sizes and shapes, and a mismatch will leak even when new. The reliable approach is to bring the old one with you or to note the brand on the flush valve. Avoid chemical tank tablets, which can attack the rubber and shorten its life.

When to call a plumber

If a new flapper does not stop the leak, the seat that the flapper rests on may be pitted or the fill valve may be worn. Both are quick fixes for a plumber, and our toilet repair service handles them. If the toilet is leaking at the base or rocking, that is a different job and worth a visit soon.

Flappers and Wethersfield water

Wethersfield's water comes from the Metropolitan District Commission. MDC's 2025 water quality report says the water is treated with chlorine, and MDC states that its water has a hardness of about 1 grain per gallon, which is soft.

Chlorine can slowly wear rubber parts, so a flapper in a house on public water can harden over a number of years. Soft water means mineral scale is less likely to foul the seal, so a flapper that fails here is more often just old rubber. Replace it, and check it again at the first sign of a running toilet.

Other parts that make a toilet run

The flapper gets the blame, but three other parts cause the same symptom. The fill valve controls how much water enters the tank. When it wears out, it can hiss, fill slowly or fail to shut off, and water then spills into the overflow tube. The float sets the water level, and if it rides too high the tank overfills. The flush valve seat is the ring the flapper rests on, and a pitted or crusted seat leaks even when the flapper is new.

A simple way to sort these out is to look at the water level. If the level sits at the top of the overflow tube, the fill valve or float is the problem. If the level drops slowly and the tank refills in short bursts, the flapper or the seat is leaking. Rubbing the seat with a fingertip tells you a lot. A smooth ring is fine, and a rough or gritty one needs cleaning with a nylon pad or a seat repair kit.

What a running toilet costs you

A toilet that runs steadily can waste a surprising amount of water over a month, and it never shows up as a visible leak. The loss goes straight down the drain, so you only see it on the bill. A repair that costs a few dollars in parts pays for itself quickly. It also protects the fill valve from running nonstop, which wears it out sooner.

Code and permit note

Swapping a flapper is routine maintenance covered by no permit. If you go further and replace the toilet or move its drain, the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), adopted by Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code, applies to that work.

Questions about toilet flappers

A flapper commonly lasts several years, but chlorinated water, tank cleaners and mineral film can shorten it. When a toilet starts to run, the flapper is the first part to check.

Wiping the flapper and its seat can restore the seal if the problem is film or grit. A flapper that is warped or hard needs replacing.
