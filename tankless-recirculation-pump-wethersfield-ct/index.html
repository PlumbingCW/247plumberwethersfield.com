Tankless Water Heater Recirculation: Wethersfield, CT Guide

A recirculation setup ends the wait for hot water at a tankless heater. Here are the options and what each costs you.

Wethersfield guideLocal facts
Talk to a plumber
(860) 397-8103Call now

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

A tankless water heater makes hot water on demand, but the water in the pipes between the heater and the faucet goes cold between uses. The wait for hot water at a distant tap is the number one complaint with tankless systems, and a recirculation setup is the usual fix.

This guide explains the main options, what they need and what they cost in energy and complexity, so you can ask a plumber the right questions.

Why the wait happens

Hot water travels slowly through long pipe runs. The cold water sitting in the line has to be pushed out first. A tankless heater also needs a minimum flow to fire, so a trickle at a sink may produce lukewarm water. A recirculation setup keeps hot water moving or ready in the lines.

The main options

Recirculation options
OptionHow it worksTrade-off
Dedicated return lineA pump circulates water through a loop back to the heaterBest performance, needs a return pipe
Crossover valveA small pump pushes cooled water back through the cold lineNo new pipe, but warms the cold line
Built-in recirculationSome tankless units have a pump and a small buffer insideSimple, but check the unit supports it
Point-of-use heaterA small electric heater near a distant tapSolves one location, adds electric load

Control matters

Timer

Runs the pump only during the hours you use hot water.

Temperature sensor

Starts the pump when the line cools below a set point.

On-demand button

Runs the pump for a short time when you press a button or use an app.

Always on

The simplest, and the most wasteful of energy.

What it means in a house like yours

The town's 2013 plan describes Wethersfield's housing as weighted toward single-family detached homes, and most have basements. In a basement-heater house, the farthest bathroom can be a long run away. A return line is easiest when the walls are open during a remodel, and a crossover valve is the usual choice when they are not.

Our tankless page explains the heater side of the system, and our installation service covers the pump, the valves and the pipe. We serve Newington and Hartford as well as Wethersfield.

Soft water and a recirculation loop

MDC says its water has a hardness of about 1 grain per gallon. A recirculation loop keeps hot water moving through the pipes and the heater, so scale matters more where the water is hard.

In soft water, scale builds slowly in the loop, which is good news for the pump and the heat exchanger. A flush on the manufacturer's schedule is still the right habit, and a loop adds a part that can wear. Ask your plumber how the pump is serviced.

Source type: MDC water hardness FAQ, Wethersfield 2013 Plan of Conservation and Development.

How much energy a loop uses

A recirculation loop moves water, and water that moves loses heat in the pipes. A loop that runs all day keeps the pipes warm and sends a steady trickle of heat into the house. Timers, temperature sensors and on demand buttons cut that loss by running the pump only when you need hot water. Insulating the hot line shrinks the loss further.

The pump itself draws little power, but the heat the loop pulls from the heater adds up. A household that showers in the morning and evening does fine with a timer set to those windows. A household with irregular hours may prefer a button or a motion sensor in the bathroom.

What to ask before you buy

  • Does my heater support a recirculation pump, and does the warranty still apply.
  • Do I need a return line, or will a crossover valve work.
  • Where will the pump go, and how is it serviced.
  • What controls come with it, and can I change the schedule.
  • What happens to my cold water at the farthest tap.

A good installer answers these before quoting. A vague answer on the warranty or the controls is a reason to ask again, because a recirculation setup that fights the heater costs more in the long run than the wait it removes.

Signs your loop needs attention

The pump runs but the water is not warm

A weak pump, air in the line or a failed check valve can be the cause.

The cold tap runs warm

A crossover valve can warm the cold line, and a stuck valve makes it worse.

A new noise from the pump

Air or a worn bearing can cause humming or rattling.

Your energy bill creeps up

A pump set to always on, or an uninsulated line, wastes heat.

A loop is a small system with a pump, a valve and a control, and a bit of attention keeps it working for years. Note the settings after install, take a photo of the pump label and keep the manual with the heater papers.

Each of these has a simple first check. Bleed air from the loop at the highest point, confirm the timer is set the way you want and feel the hot line to see how far the heat reaches. If the line is warm only part of the way, a valve or the pump itself may have failed.

Code and permit note

Adding a pump and a return line is plumbing work. The 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), adopted by Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code, covers pipe, insulation and valve requirements, and permit rules vary with the work.
