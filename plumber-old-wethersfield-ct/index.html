Plumber in Old Wethersfield, Wethersfield, CT

Old Wethersfield is the town's original village, settled in 1634. This page covers what to know about plumbing for homes in and around its historic district.

Old Wethersfield

The village and where it sits

The town's 2013 plan dates the village's settlement to 1634 and calls its historic district the first and largest local historic district in Connecticut.

The village sits along the Connecticut River at the north end of town. Public reference sources describe it as roughly bounded by the Hartford and Rocky Hill borders, the railroad tracks and Interstate 91. We have not found an official boundary map, so we describe a job by its street address and not by a line on a map.

What the record says about the houses

The state's local historic district directory lists the Old Wethersfield Historic District as established in 1962 and counts more than 150 surviving structures built before 1850. Main Street holds the Webb-Deane-Stevens Museum houses, including the Silas Deane House at 203 Main Street, and the town's visitor brochure describes Broad Street Green as surrounded by old homes and large trees.

Those are museum buildings and not a statement about anyone's pipes. A house can be two centuries old and have had its plumbing replaced twice, or it can be younger and still carry something original. That is why we ask about the house first: age tells a plumber where to look and not what is there. We look at what is actually behind the wall and explain what we find before anything is replaced.

Work from several eras

A house that has been updated over generations can show different kinds of pipe in one basement. Ask the plumber to trace which lines are which before agreeing to replace everything.

Plaster and tile behind the leak

Opening finished walls in an older house takes care. Leak detection that locates the source first can limit how much has to be cut. See leak detection for how that works.

Tight spaces for a water heater

Basements and closets in older houses can be small. Check clearances, venting and the route out for the old tank before you choose a replacement.

A shutoff nobody has touched

Find your main shutoff before you need it. A valve that has not moved in years can stick, and it is better to learn that on a dry day.

Historic district rules and plumbing work

Old Wethersfield has a local Historic District Commission. Most plumbing happens inside walls and basements, but vents, exterior fixtures, meter boxes and utility lines can touch the outside of a building. We have not confirmed which kinds of work the commission reviews, so ask the town planning office before you change anything visible from the street.

Permits and inspections for plumbing work go through the Wethersfield Building Department at 505 Silas Deane Highway, phone 860-721-2840. Permit rules vary with the work, so confirm yours with the department.

Water, sewer and the Cove

Water and sewer service in Wethersfield come from MDC, the Metropolitan District. MDC's Clean Water Project page for the town says most of Wethersfield's 98 miles of sewer main are more than 50 years old and that some pipe dates to 1914. It also says heavy rain pushes extra water into the sewers, which can back sewage into basements and spill into Wethersfield Cove and the Connecticut River.

That is a town-wide statement and not a finding about any one street. If sewage comes up a basement floor drain, treat the cause as unknown until it is checked. A camera inspection shows whether the blockage is in your line or in the street main, and the street main is MDC's to fix. See sewer camera inspection and drain cleaning.

The Cove is an oxbow of the river about 10 feet above sea level, according to the town's visitor brochure. We have not assessed flood risk for any street. If your basement is low, a working sump pump and a clear discharge path are worth checking. MDC's page names homeowner connections such as downspouts and sump pumps as one source of the extra water in sewers, so ask MDC what is allowed before you change a discharge.

Before you call: four quick checks

1

Find the main shutoff

Look near where the water line enters the house, often in the basement. Know which way the valve turns.

2

Note what you see

Write down which fixtures are affected, whether water is moving and when it started. A photo helps.

3

Protect what you can

Move rugs, boxes and electronics off a wet floor. Keep power off to wet areas only if the panel is dry and easy to reach.

4

Have the address and age ready

The street address, the approximate age of the house and any past plumbing work help the plumber plan.
