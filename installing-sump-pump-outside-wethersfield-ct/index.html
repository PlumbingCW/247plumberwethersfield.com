Installing a Sump Pump Outside: Wethersfield, CT Basics

A sump pump sits in a pit inside the house but it has to discharge outside. Here is how the system is set up and where the water should go.

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

Installing a sump pump outside usually means two things. The pump and pit go in the lowest part of the basement, and the discharge pipe runs through the wall to the yard. The water has to go away from the foundation and not back into the sewer. That second point matters more than most homeowners expect.

This guide covers how the parts fit together, where the discharge line should end and what to check so the pump works when a storm hits. It also explains what a plumber handles and what a homeowner can do.

The parts of the system

Pit and liner

A basin cut into the floor that collects groundwater.

Pump

A submersible pump sits inside. A pedestal pump sits above the pit.

Float switch

Starts the pump when the water rises and stops it when the pit is empty.

Check valve

Stops water in the discharge pipe from flowing back into the pit.

Discharge pipe

Carries water out through the rim joist to the yard.

Where the discharge should go

The outlet should deliver water well away from the foundation, so it does not flow right back to the pit. Many installers extend the pipe several feet from the wall and slope the yard to carry water off. A pipe that ends against the house recycles the same water. A pipe that ends near a neighbor's property can cause a dispute.

Install steps in order

1

Pick the low point

Place the pit where water collects first, usually the lowest corner.

2

Cut the floor and set the liner

Break out the slab, dig the pit and set the basin level.

3

Set the pump and the check valve

Place the pump on a flat base and add a check valve in the discharge line.

4

Run the discharge pipe

Go through the rim joist with a sleeve, slope away and finish with a screen at the outlet.

5

Wire it safely

Use a dedicated outlet with ground fault protection, and test by pouring water into the pit.

Add a backup

Storms cut power, and a sump pump with no power does nothing. A battery backup or a water powered backup keeps the pit from overflowing during an outage. Test the pump twice a year, and keep the pit clear of debris.

Why the discharge must not go to the sewer

MDC's Clean Water Project is removing homeowner drainage connections from the sanitary sewer, including downspouts, sump pumps and yard drains. MDC says the flow in the system rises from about 1.7 million gallons a day to about 9.1 million in rain, and that overflows can reach basements and Wethersfield Cove.

The practical point is simple. A sump pump that discharges to the sewer adds to the surge that backs up basements, perhaps your own. Route the discharge to the yard, and ask MDC if you are unsure how your house is connected.

Source type: MDC Clean Water Project, Wethersfield page.

Choosing the right pump

Pumps come in two main styles. A submersible pump sits inside the pit, runs quietly and handles higher flows. A pedestal pump has its motor above the pit, which keeps it cooler and easier to reach, but it is louder and the pit has to be narrower. Most homes with finished basements use submersible pumps.

Horsepower and lift matter. The pump has to raise water from the pit to the outlet and push it through the discharge pipe, and a pump that is too small runs constantly and wears out. The right size depends on the depth of the pit, the height of the outlet and how much water the house takes on in the worst storms. A plumber sizes it by looking at all three.

Maintenance that prevents failures

  • Test the pump twice a year by pouring water into the pit.
  • Clean the pit of silt, gravel and debris.
  • Check that the float moves freely and does not stick to the wall.
  • Confirm the discharge outlet is clear of ice, leaves and soil.
  • Replace the pump when it is old, since most do not last forever.

A pump that has not run for months can seize. A test before storm season catches that early, when a replacement is a simple visit and not an emergency in a flooded basement.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Placing the pit away from the spot where water collects first.
  • Leaving out the check valve, so the pump short cycles.
  • Ending the discharge pipe against the foundation.
  • Connecting the discharge to a sanitary drain.
  • Forgetting a ground fault outlet and a plan for power outages.

Each of these is easy to avoid at install time and annoying to fix later. A short conversation with the installer about all five before the work starts is time well spent.

Code and permit note

A new sump pit and discharge line is plumbing work that falls under the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), adopted by Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code. Permit rules vary with the work, so call the Building Department at 860-721-2840 first.
