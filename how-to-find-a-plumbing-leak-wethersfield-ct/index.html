How to Find a Plumbing Leak in Your Wethersfield, CT Home

To find a leak, first prove you have one with the water meter, then narrow it down fixture by fixture. Here is the order that works.

Written by the Wethersfield 24/7 Plumbing team. Published and updated November 14, 2026.

Most people find a leak by accident: a stain on a ceiling, a musty smell, a higher bill. Finding where it is takes a method. The fastest approach is to confirm the leak with the water meter, split the house into zones and test each one, then look at the usual suspects.

This guide walks through that order. It also tells you when to stop searching and bring in a plumber with listening gear, because some leaks are not findable by eye.

Step one: confirm a leak with the meter

1

Turn everything off

Make sure no one is using water. Stop the dishwasher, the washing machine and any ice maker cycle.

2

Read the meter

Find the water meter, usually in the basement near where the line enters, and note the dial or the small leak indicator.

3

Wait

Leave everything off for fifteen to thirty minutes without using water.

4

Read it again

If the dial or leak indicator moved, water is escaping somewhere in the system.

Step two: check the usual suspects

Toilets

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank. If color shows in the bowl without flushing, the flapper leaks.

Water heater

Look at the base and the relief valve pipe for drips and rust.

Under sinks

Run a dry paper towel along every supply line and trap.

Outdoor faucets and hoses

A hose left attached or a worn spigot can leak for months.

Irrigation and softener lines

Systems that tie into the supply are easy to forget.

Step three: split the house

If the meter still moves after the checks, close the main valve and watch the meter. If it stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the main valve, which is a service line problem. Then open the main again and close branch valves one at a time. When the meter stops after closing a given valve, you have found the zone.

Signs of a hidden leak

  • Warm spots on the floor, which can mean a hot water line under the slab.
  • A musty smell with no visible cause.
  • Paint or wallpaper that bubbles.
  • The sound of running water with everything off.
  • Unexplained rises in the water bill.

When to stop searching

Leaks in a slab, in a wall or in an underground service line cannot be found by looking. A plumber with acoustic, thermal and tracer tools can narrow the spot, and our leak detection service does that. If the leak turns out to be a failed line, water service line repair or repiping is the fix. We serve Newington and Hartford along with Wethersfield, and the emergency line is answered 24/7.

Hidden leaks that give themselves away

Some leaks leave clues long before they leave puddles. A hot water line leaking under a slab can warm a patch of the floor, and the warmth shows up as a spot where bare feet feel the difference. A drain leak behind a wall can leave a smell like damp soil. A slow supply leak near a foundation can stain the base of a wall and raise the humidity in the room.

Sound is a clue too. Put your ear to a wall or a floor with every fixture off. A faint hiss or a trickle can mean a pressurized line is leaking. If the water heater makes noise while no one is using hot water, a leak in a hot line may be the reason.

When the leak is in the walls

A stain that grows

A ceiling spot that spreads after use of the room above points to a drain or supply line.

A stain that appears only after rain

That points to the roof or the siding, not the plumbing.

A stain below a bathroom

Check the tub or shower drain and the supply to the vanity.

A stain below a kitchen

Check the dishwasher line, the sink trap and the ice maker supply.

Do not cut into a wall or ceiling to look. A plumber can find the source with moisture meters and thermal imaging and open only the spot that needs it.

Code and permit note

Looking for a leak needs no permit. Fixing one on a supply line does fall under the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), as adopted by Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code, and a plumber uses it as the reference for the repair.
